This is worth a bounded feasibility pilot. It is not ready for a production commitment or a staffing-reduction promise. Extraction and routing are plausible uses; severity classification needs particular care because a missed urgent claim can matter far more than a wrong queue.

Your baseline implies 18,000 minutes, or 300 staff-hours, per week: about 15,600 hours annually if volumes hold for 52 weeks. Across 14 people, that is roughly 21.4 hours per person per week on this activity. It does not establish that 14 jobs can be removed. A hypothetical 50% net time saving would release 150 hours weekly before implementation, operating costs, and any additional review burden. Measure loaded labor cost and how released capacity would actually be used before claiming an ROI.

Four years of records are promising, but final assignments and outcomes are not automatically reliable labels for initial triage. Assignments may reflect capacity or later information unavailable at intake; rules may have changed. Keep final outcomes out of model inputs when they would not exist at the decision time. Check historical attachments, OCR quality, missing fields, languages, label consistency, and retention permissions before assuming the data are usable.

This month, appoint the head of claims as accountable business owner, an engineering owner, and an adjuster lead. Interview and observe the people doing triage, including adjusters, to establish actual failure modes, exception handling, and acceptable review workload. Bring privacy and security owners into the design because the records contain personal health information and the deployment environment is on-premises. Confirm approved processing locations, access, retention, logging, and model deployment options before exposing any records to a service.

Compare three approaches on the same representative data: existing manual work, OCR plus rules or conventional classification, and an LLM-assisted workflow. Start with key-field extraction and proposed routing, showing source passages and allowing corrections. Preserve manual fallback. Keep urgency decisions under meaningful human review until evidence supports changing that boundary; do not let the system deny claims or decide coverage as an incidental expansion of triage.

Build a held-out, preferably later-time test set separated from prompt development, with claim families kept together to prevent duplicates leaking across splits. Experienced adjusters should independently label and adjudicate ambiguous cases. Include rare urgent claims, poor scans, unusual formats, conflicting attachments, and malicious instructions embedded in documents. Treat incoming content as data, never as permission to change workflow controls.

Measure field accuracy, routing errors, urgent-claim recall and false negatives, performance by claim category, abstention rates, reviewer corrections, total handling time, rework, and cost per claim. Agree acceptable thresholds with claims leadership before evaluating, weighting consequential mistakes more heavily than overall accuracy. A model's stated confidence alone is not a release gate.

After offline evaluation, run shadow mode without changing queues, then a small assisted pilot if privacy, quality, and net time-saving gates pass. Stop or redesign if urgent cases are missed beyond the agreed tolerance, review eliminates the savings, or the approved infrastructure cannot support the workflow. Give the pilot a fixed budget, an accountable decision-maker, and a review date; do not force production deployment to meet the board presentation.

For the board next month, present this as an evidence-led AI candidate: baseline workload, approved data boundary, pilot scope and cost, success and stop criteria, and when the investment decision will return. The recommendation is to fund learning now; rollout depends on demonstrated benefit and controlled harm.
