# Sourcing decision: Engineering capacity

Engineering leader · Evidence: your description

## Recommendation

**Confidence: High that the proposed team size is unsupported; low on the eventual staffing route.**

Do not commit to five or six agency developers yet. First establish what is limiting delivery and free an accountable technical owner. Fifteen stretched engineers and slipping roadmap dates are evidence of pressure, not proof that more developers will resolve it. A nearshore team can be useful if there is ready work and internal capacity to direct, review and accept it; those conditions are currently unknown.

## Why this choice

Your preferred option buys delivery capacity while leaving product decisions, technical integration and operational accountability with you. If existing staff are waiting on decisions, overloaded with work in progress or blocked in review, adding developers can deepen the constraint. If the backlog contains well-understood implementation work and review capacity exists, augmentation becomes credible.

The strongest immediate alternative is reprioritization: reduce concurrent commitments and inspect recent slipped work for blocked time, unplanned support, rework and decision latency. This is a proposed diagnostic, not a claim about what is wrong. Continuing the current roadmap unchanged preserves overload and makes the next commitment difficult to trust.

Compare hiring if the capability need is durable and funding, management and recruiting evidence support it. A project partner may suit a separable accepted outcome, but cannot replace a missing internal decision maker. Buying a product matters only if a specific roadmap component is a mature utility; there is no component evidence yet.

## Cost, transition and exit

Use the same prioritized outcomes and delivery horizon when comparing options. Agency fees need internal direction, onboarding, access, review, rework and handover effort added. Hiring requires recruiting, ramp, compensation and manager time. Existing employees are not free capacity: identify which work moves if they absorb the priority.

The nearshore location alone establishes neither cost advantage nor usable overlap. Verify communication hours and time to productive, accepted work. Name an internal technical lead and the people who will maintain the result; allocate their time before a contract. Review an engagement at accepted increments and end or resize it when the capacity gap closes or coordination costs defeat the benefit. Transfer is complete when the receiving team can change, deploy and recover the work.

## Next action

Proposed owner: the engineering manager with the product lead. Before the next staffing decision, inspect a bounded set of recent slips and map the highest-priority backlog to skills, ready work and review capacity. Produce the binding constraint and a comparison of one plausible staffing route with reprioritization. Approve a small, reversible engagement only if that evidence shows it addresses the constraint; team size should follow the work.

## What could change the recommendation

An immediately ready backlog, a lead with allocated review time and a temporary demand spike would favor augmentation. Three questions matter: where does slipped work wait; what sustained skill/duration gap exists; and who has time to direct additional staff? Those facts separate a capacity purchase from an expensive increase in work in progress.
